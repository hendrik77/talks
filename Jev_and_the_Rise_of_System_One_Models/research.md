# Jev and "System One" Models: Decisions in Milliseconds

## 1. Purpose and scope

This document is the knowledge base for the speaker notes of the talk and the live demo. It summarises the newsletter, adds background and counter-positions, and translates the topic into concrete implications for our work as software consultants: architecture decisions in client projects, advising clients on build-vs-buy and compliance, and our own tooling.

## 2. Summary of the newsletter

The c't 3003 newsletter describes Jev as a new kind of language model that can do only one thing: make decisions. It answers yes/no questions, rates on a scale, or picks from a predefined set of options. Because all questions are answered at once rather than generated token by token, it is very fast [1]. The vendor's claim that Jev "mathematically cannot" hallucinate is called largely marketing by the author: if the only permitted outputs are "billing", "technical" or "sales", nothing else can come out, but that does not guarantee the answer is correct. Answers are returned as JSON together with a probability of being right. Pricing is extremely low at USD 0.042 per million input tokens, with output free [1].

Jev comes from TypeSafe AI in San Francisco, which left stealth on 15 September with USD 40 million in seed funding. The model was built by Diogo Almeida, who worked on InstructGPT and RLHF at OpenAI. TypeSafe calls Jev a "System One model" after Daniel Kahneman's fast thinking, and trains it with "RLCD" (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions); the goal is a model that is right 70 % of the time when it says it is 70 % confident. Parameter count and architecture are not disclosed; the competitive edge is said to be fully synthetic training data [1].

The newsletter stresses that the idea is not new: researcher Nandakishor Mukkunnoth published "SalesRLAgent" in March 2025, including paper, weights and dataset, under an unmarketable Hugging Face name. Three days after the Jev launch he released Laya: Apache 2.0, 421 million parameters, under 1 GB of weights, installable via pip, about 33 ms per decision on an old Tesla T4, 13 ms on an M3 Max via an MLX port and under 5 ms via the Apple Neural Engine. The model card admits that without fine-tuning Laya is barely better than chance. Within a week an "awesome list" collected around 25 open re-implementations, runtimes, benchmarks and calibration tools [1].

On performance, the newsletter reports a phishing test on 2,000 synthetic e-mails: with one question Jev reaches 62.6 %, Claude Haiku 4.5 81.3 %, with Jev about three times faster at one twelfth of the cost. Split into five questions and weighted using 1,000 labelled e-mails, Jev reaches 95 % on the other 1,000, Haiku 93.2 % with the same five questions, and a two-line regex 91.8 %. In a comparison of 791 intent decisions Jev is about five points behind GPT-5.6 Terra and roughly level with small models such as GPT-5.4 nano, but three times faster and 1.5 instead of 61 cents per 1,000 decisions; putting Jev in front and escalating only uncertain cases to Terra yields Terra-level accuracy at a quarter of the cost. A DistilBERT fine-tuned on up to 10,000 examples beats Jev on AG News and Banking77 in "JevBench" and answers locally in under 10 ms instead of about 380 ms [1].

On calibration, the newsletter cites an independent test: on benchmarks probably seen in training, stated confidence deviates from real accuracy by about 2.5 points, on unfamiliar support tickets by almost 11. On a priority rule that was not inferable from the text, Jev was right only 44.7 % of the time while claiming 74 % confidence on average. A pre-registered evaluation found Jev reliable only at confidence 0.99, where it was always right (60 % of requests), while accuracy between 0.5 and 0.95 was nearly flat [1].

Community demos include a "slop detector" browser extension, a Minecraft agent (GPT-6 Astra plans, Jev steers with 131 decisions, total model cost 97 cents of which 1 cent was Jev), a browser-use "jev-ultrafast" mode (flight search Zurich–London in seven seconds for 0.4 cents), "jevmail" (Gmail triage, about 1,000 mails per minute for about 3 cents), and "fast-jev-compaction", a Claude Code plugin that decides which tool calls are stale [1].

The author's take: at its core Jev reduces an AI task to a single decision and leaves everything else to the large model; with a clean harness and the right data you can get there yourself. The confidence value can lull users into false security, because a number without traceable calibration is just a number, whereas with a reasoning model you can at least read why a decision was made. The author worries about what this means for systems in public administration or the military. Still, the demo week showed that the gap for fast, typed decisions next to a large model is real; whether Jev or just the idea survives remains open [1].

## 3. What Jev is: interface, primitives, economics

Technically, Jev is exposed as a single endpoint to which the client posts a block of state, a model identifier and a map of questions; the documentation defines three question types, which are evaluated in parallel and in isolation against the same state, and a Choice question can have up to 255 options [4]. The three primitives are Choice (pick one option), Score (a level on a scale) and "Noul" (a yes/no probability) [5]. TypeSafe reports 70 to 500 ms end-to-end latency and prices input at USD 0.042 per million tokens with free output [5], which matches the newsletter [1].

A useful mental model from a developer write-up is that Jev behaves like a "smart if statement": ordinary code branches on computable values, but breaks down when the condition is a judgement such as "is this customer angry?" [36]. Vercel's explainer makes the division of labour explicit: the application defines the possible answers and decides whether to act on the result; Jev only supplies the judgement [10]. The name "System One" refers to Kahneman's distinction between fast, intuitive and slow, deliberate thinking [10], while the model name itself points to William Stanley Jevons and his paradox that cheaper resources lead to higher total consumption, i.e. the bet that decisions will be made far more often once they are cheap enough [5].

A further framing from the agent-platform vendor Beam is that most steps in a production agent are not writing but deciding (routing a ticket, classifying a document, checking whether input is safe, choosing a tool), and these steps have so far been handled with text generators that occasionally invent tools that do not exist [32].

## 4. Company, founder, training

TypeSafe AI was founded in 2024 by Diogo Almeida, Erik Gafni and Sasha Sheng; Almeida spent about four years at OpenAI working on RLHF, InstructGPT, ChatGPT and GPT-4 [7]. The USD 40 million seed round was led by DCVC and announced on launch day, 15 September 2026 [6]; Wikipedia, citing Forbes, reports a valuation of USD 200 million [7] (secondary source; the Forbes original was not checked). Almeida told TechCrunch that his motivation was disappointment with conversational models: "We have lightning in a bottle, and yet it is not useful." [2]. According to TechCrunch, he describes the training as reinforcement learning on calibrated decisions using exclusively synthetic data, and calls the early bet on generating all data in-house one of the best of his life; TypeSafe plans further versions in new modalities [2].

The architecture is undisclosed; outside observers suspect an open-weight language model as the base, and TypeSafe concedes that it cannot prove its prices are not subsidised [33][4]. The most detailed outside attempt is Archer Hume's "Jev's Architecture Unmasked", which probed the API with about 10,000 calls and infers that Jev keeps a pretrained LLM, encodes the shared state once, builds all questions in parallel without letting them see each other, and reads answer probabilities from internal representations via outcome-trained readouts instead of generating text [49]. Hume inferred this from black-box measurements; TypeSafe has not confirmed it. This matters for consultants: we cannot currently assess cost stability, model provenance or supply-chain risk.

## 5. Background: InstructGPT and RLHF at OpenAI

To understand what TypeSafe is reacting against, it helps to know the technique its founder helped build. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** goes back to a 2017 paper by Christiano et al., a collaboration between OpenAI and DeepMind. Its starting point was a practical problem: for many tasks it is hard to write down a reward function that says what "good" behaviour is. Instead, non-expert humans compared pairs of short clips of an agent's behaviour and said which one was better. A model learned the goal from these preferences, and a separate reinforcement-learning step learned the behaviour to reach that goal; with feedback on only about 0.1 % of the agent's interactions, this was enough to solve Atari games and simulated robot locomotion tasks [37]. The key design idea, separating "learn what humans want" from "learn how to do it", is what later carried over to language models.

**InstructGPT** applied this idea to GPT-3. The paper "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" was submitted to arXiv on 4 March 2022 by Long Ouyang and 19 co-authors; Diogo Almeida is listed as the fourth author [38]. The authors start from the observation that making language models bigger does not by itself make them better at following a user's intent: large models can produce untruthful, toxic or simply unhelpful output [38]. Their method has three stages. First, human labelers write demonstrations of the desired behaviour for prompts (written by labelers or submitted through the OpenAI API), and GPT-3 is fine-tuned on them with supervised learning. Second, labelers rank several model outputs for the same prompt, and this comparison data is used to further fine-tune the model with RLHF [38]. In more detail, the rankings train a separate reward model that predicts which answer humans would prefer, and the language model is then optimised against this reward model with the reinforcement-learning algorithm Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [40].

The results made the approach famous. In human evaluations, outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model were preferred over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, despite being 100 times smaller; InstructGPT also improved on truthfulness and produced less toxic output, with only small regressions on public NLP benchmarks, although it still made simple mistakes [38]. **ChatGPT** followed in November 2022: OpenAI's announcement stated that it was trained with RLHF using the same methods as InstructGPT, with slight differences in data collection [39]. Since then RLHF and its successors have become standard in leading language models, and the field has broadened into "preference fine-tuning", including methods such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) that skip the separate reward model [39].

**Why this matters for Jev.** The connection is more than biography. RLHF optimises a model for what human raters prefer to read, and Almeida's critique targets exactly that: in Forbes he said the industry has become superhuman at pleasing humans [7], and he told TechCrunch that the problem is optimising for human language, which leaves models awkward inside software [6]. TypeSafe explicitly positions Jev as a response to overconfidence in existing models [7]. The name of its training method, RLCD, echoes RLHF: reinforcement learning is still the tool, but the signal is described as calibrated decisions rather than human preferences, and the training data is fully synthetic instead of human-labelled [2]. TypeSafe has not published how RLCD works, so the comparison with RLHF is our interpretation. SalesRLAgent, the prior art discussed in section 9, likewise used PPO, the same algorithm family as InstructGPT, to learn a probability output [13].

For consultants, this background gives a useful one-sentence explanation for clients: chat models are trained to produce answers people like, while decision models such as Jev are trained to produce answers software can act on; whether the latter's probabilities deserve trust is an empirical question that has to be tested on the client's own data (see section 12).

## 6. Background: Kahneman's System 1 and System 2

TypeSafe's product category, "System One model", borrows its name from psychology [10]. Because the metaphor shapes how clients will understand the product, this section explains what it originally meant, where it fits Jev surprisingly well, and where it breaks down.

**The original distinction.** Daniel Kahneman, psychologist and 2002 Nobel laureate in economics [46], popularised the distinction in his 2011 book *Thinking, Fast and Slow* [42], which summarises decades of research he conducted with Amos Tversky. The labels "System 1" and "System 2" were not his invention; he adopted them from the psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West [44]. In Kahneman's definition, System 1 works automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and without a sense of voluntary control, while System 2 directs attention to effortful mental work such as complex computations and is associated with the experience of agency, choice and concentration [43]. His examples for System 1 include judging that one object is farther away than another, turning towards a sudden sound, answering 2 + 2, reading words and understanding simple sentences [44]. System 2 examples include focusing on one voice in a noisy room, counting how often the letter "a" appears on a page, and checking whether a complex logical argument is valid; what they share is that they need attention and break down when attention is drawn away [43].

**How the two interact.** The systems are not independent. System 1 continuously produces impressions and feelings that become the raw material for the explicit beliefs and deliberate choices of System 2; System 1 can generate surprisingly complex patterns of ideas, but only the slower System 2 can build a thought step by step in an orderly sequence [43]. One of System 2's jobs is to override the impulses of System 1, which is why Kahneman places self-control there [43]. Most of the time, however, System 2 simply accepts what System 1 suggests.

**Where the metaphor fits Jev well.** The mapping to a software architecture is almost literal. Jev plays System 1: it returns a fast, cheap impression (a choice, a score, a probability) without a visible chain of reasoning [5]. The surrounding code, a large reasoning model or a human reviewer plays System 2: it decides whether to accept the impression, and the confidence threshold is the trigger that "calls in" System 2 [11]. Even the weaknesses line up. Kahneman lists counting the letter "a" on a page as a typical System 2 task [43], and TypeSafe's own documentation says Jev cannot reliably count characters, occurrences or list items and struggles with numeric precision, so arithmetic should stay in code [36][3]. TypeSafe itself tells developers to hand System 2 tasks to something other than its System 1 model.

**The most useful part for us: when can intuition be trusted?** Kahneman's most practical contribution here is not the book but a 2009 paper with Gary Klein, a researcher who had studied expert intuition in fields such as firefighting. Starting from the observation that professional intuition is sometimes excellent and sometimes badly flawed, they concluded that the quality of an intuitive judgement depends on how predictable the environment is and whether the person had the opportunity to learn its regularities; subjective confidence, they warned, is not a reliable indicator of accuracy [45]. In a later McKinsey interview Kahneman added that he was concerned about overconfidence in decisions that are unique rather than repetitive, and that experts often do not know where the boundaries of their expertise lie [46].

This maps remarkably well onto the independent Jev evaluations. On familiar, regular tasks its confidence tracked accuracy closely, while on unfamiliar support tickets with a priority rule it had never seen, Jev was right 44.7 % of the time but claimed 74 % confidence on average [1][19]. That is exactly the failure mode Kahneman and Klein describe for human intuition outside a "high-validity" environment. The consequence for architecture is the same as for people: trust the fast judgement only where the task is regular and where you have feedback (labelled data) to check it, and route everything else to slow, deliberate processing.

**Where the metaphor breaks down.** Two caveats belong in any client conversation. First, the dual-system model is contested in psychology itself. Melnikoff and Bargh argued in 2018 that the division of mental processes into two types lacks empirical support, contradicts established findings and is internally incoherent, calling it a convenient and seductive myth [47]. Pennycook and colleagues replied that this critique targets a list-of-features view that current dual-process theorists have already abandoned [48]. The debate is unresolved, so "System 1" is best treated as a helpful metaphor. Second, the label says nothing about how Jev works internally: its architecture is undisclosed [33], and a "System One model" is a product category defined by its interface (typed, fast, single-pass decisions); the label makes no claim about resemblance to the brain.

**Relevance for consultants.** The vocabulary is useful precisely because clients already know it. It offers a simple triage question for every AI use case: is this a System-1 decision (high volume, repetitive, stable categories, feedback available) or a System-2 task (novel, multi-step, requiring explanation)? Combined with the Kahneman–Klein test (Is the environment regular? Is there feedback to learn from and to verify against? How costly is overconfidence?), this becomes a lightweight checklist for deciding where a decision model belongs and where a human or a reasoning model must stay in the loop. This checklist is our own synthesis and has not been validated.

## 7. Launch dynamics and ecosystem

Adoption was unusually fast. According to The Next Web, Jev reached 13 % of Vercel's paid teams within 24 hours, the fastest adoption in the history of Vercel's AI gateway [6]. Vercel added Jev to its AI Gateway on 16 September and shipped an `evaluate` method in AI SDK 7, including a zero-data-retention option [5]. Within 48 hours developers had shipped a coding-agent guardrail, an MCP server and an open-weights clone of the interface [5]. TypeSafe opened sign-ups to everyone on 20 September with USD 5 free credit and paused new sign-ups on 22 September because of demand [5]; the API also briefly stopped serving requests during the launch rush [33].

Reported community use cases include command-safety review (Vercel's CEO reported Jev up to 18× faster at p95 than GPT Luna and more accurate), e-mail triage, and Browser Use's "jev-ultrafast", which completed a Zurich–London flight search in 7.1 seconds [4]; these confirm the demos listed in the newsletter [1]. Community tooling for testing also appeared quickly, for example `jevassert`, which records Jev predictions and checks accuracy, calibration and cost offline and deterministically in CI [31].

## 8. "Cannot hallucinate": what the claim actually means

Several independent explainers agree with the newsletter's scepticism. The guarantee is a schema guarantee: Jev cannot return a malformed value or an option that was not declared, but it can still choose the wrong valid option, and TypeSafe's own FAQ acknowledges this [9]. MarkTechPost notes that TypeSafe's "0 % hallucination" figure is not an empirical measurement but follows from guaranteed schema matching [4]. A large independent study found zero invalid answers across 23,703 decision-model calls, which confirms the structural property [20]. One blogger adds an important consequence: a wrong answer from a valid set is harder to notice than a malformed one, because nothing downstream fails [30].

TypeSafe deserves credit for publishing a "jaggedness" page listing known weaknesses of jev-1.13 (reviewed 17 September 2026): it struggles with extra levels of indirection, reads instructions literally, and is weak at numeric precision; it answers the question as written rather than as meant, and score outputs should not be used to compute exact magnitudes [3]. One write-up counts nine documented failure modes, including susceptibility to adversarial content in the state [11]. Tom's Hardware summarises that the developer, not Jev, is responsible for acting on confidence values, and that Jev can misclassify, be attacked adversarially, or answer the literal wording rather than the meaning [8].

## 9. Prior art and open alternatives

**SalesRLAgent.** The arXiv paper 2503.23303 by Nandakishor M., submitted on 30 March 2025, describes a reinforcement-learning framework that predicts conversion probability during sales conversations and was trained on synthetic data generated with GPT-4o [12]. The published Hugging Face model uses PPO via Stable Baselines3 with a continuous action space (a conversion probability between 0 and 1) [13]. This confirms the newsletter's date and repository name [1]. It is, however, a vertical sales model rather than a general decision engine, so the similarity is in the approach (non-generative, RL-trained, probability output, synthetic data), while the product scope differs.

**Laya.** Laya, released by Convai Innovations under Apache 2.0, has 421 million parameters, runs locally and returns typed decisions in about 32.8 ms [14]. The model card describes a ModernBERT-large encoder with a small decision head rather than a new architecture [14]; it is a bidirectional encoder that scores options instead of generating them [15]. The critical caveat: zero-shot it scores 0.362 where random guessing scores 0.318 and always picking the most common answer scores 0.461, and its expected calibration error of 0.466 only drops to 0.081 after per-question temperature refitting [14]. Choice questions degrade with more than 20 options [14]. Mukkunnoth frames Laya as a priority claim, pointing to his March 2025 paper and a September 2025 follow-up; the community was split, with some arguing that branding and a usable API are the actual product [15]. An independent benchmark warns that Laya's model card compares numbers from two different benchmarks, which makes its claimed "+16 % advantage" over Jev meaningless, and finds both Laya and Jev about 20 points behind Claude Haiku 4.5 on the phishing set [16].

**The broader lineage.** A Japanese analysis of seven Jev clones concludes that the clones reproduce only the computational form, and that this form has existed since around 2020 [34]. The architectural pattern of a small, cheap scorer in front of expensive LLMs is also well documented: FrugalGPT (Stanford, 2023) showed that an LLM cascade can match the best single model with up to 98 % cost reduction [24], and it used a small DistilBERT model to score whether an answer was good enough to stop the cascade [25]. What is new with Jev is the product packaging: a general-purpose, hosted, typed API with calibrated probabilities and aggressive pricing.

**The open ecosystem, nine days after launch.** Within a week, the interface stopped being one vendor's product. A curated "awesome-jev" list on GitHub collects projects, integrations and discussions around Jev [55], and one catalogue counts about 35 open alternatives; all figures there are the projects' own, on benchmarks they chose, and none has been independently re-run [52]. An Italian tech outlet summarises the common denominator: the clones copy the interface and leave TypeSafe's model aside. You pass in state, define questions and allowed options at runtime and get a probability per option, computed in a single forward pass by reading the scores for the allowed options only, without generating tokens [51]. RLCD itself has not been described in enough detail to copy, so no project reproduces it; several fit temperature scaling afterwards instead [53].

The most prominent clone is **Kev** by Jared Palmer. It is a family of small decision models (0.8B, 4B and 9B) built on Qwen3.5 and based on the architecture inferred in Hume's article; the API matches TypeSafe's, so TypeSafe's own Python SDK can be pointed at a local Kev server, and training code and evaluation data are published [50]. It runs on CUDA, ROCm and Apple Silicon, the 4B and 9B models fit on a 32 GB Mac, and a web playground (also available in the browser as a Hugging Face Space [56]) lets users test how option order affects the answers [50]. Technically, Kev combines a LoRA adapter with a small pointer head; the shared state is processed once and reused across questions [54]. On its own benchmarks Kev-9B trails Jev by 3.5 points on a development set (0.822 vs. 0.857) and scores 0.852 on a test set Jev has not been run on; the author stresses that this is not a controlled comparison because Jev's training data is unknown [50]. Calibration uses a single fitted temperature: Kev-9B gives at least 0.9 probability to a wrong answer on 4.0 % of new-source questions (Jev: 3.7 %), and the share of decisions that could be automated within a 5 % error budget (0.45–0.57) is still below Jev's 0.70 [50]. SemIf and Kev together gathered more than 4,000 GitHub stars within days [51].

Other notable projects: Laya now ships an additional 322M multilingual checkpoint covering over 100 languages, relevant for German-language client data [57]; von reports under 15 ms per decision and Laya about 86.5 decisions per second in roughly 1 GB of memory (self-reported) [52]; smaller trained models include OpenJev Verdict (151M) and NanoJev (0.6B) [52]. For a hosted alternative from another vendor, Together AI serves "Tev1", which answers only Choice questions [53]. The awesome list also shows where enthusiasm outruns judgement: several listed projects let Jev decide stock or crypto trades [55], which is exactly the "false security" scenario the newsletter warns about [1].

## 10. Background: DistilBERT and small encoder models

DistilBERT appears twice in this story: as the cheap scorer inside the FrugalGPT cascade [25] and, according to the newsletter, as the fine-tuned baseline that beats Jev on AG News and Banking77 [1]. It stands for a whole class of alternatives to Jev that many clients could run themselves.

**What it is.** DistilBERT was published by Victor Sanh, Lysandre Debut, Julien Chaumond and Thomas Wolf of Hugging Face on arXiv on 2 October 2019 [41]. It is a smaller, general-purpose language representation model derived from Google's BERT. Like BERT, it is pre-trained once on general text and then fine-tuned for a specific task such as classification [41]. Architecturally it is an *encoder*: it reads the whole input at once and turns it into a representation, rather than generating text token by token. Laya is built on the same principle, using a (much newer) ModernBERT-large encoder that scores a fixed set of options instead of writing an answer [14][15].

**How it was made: knowledge distillation.** The name comes from the training method. A large "teacher" model (BERT) supervises a smaller "student" model during pre-training, so that the student learns to imitate the teacher's behaviour. Earlier work had mostly used distillation to build task-specific models; DistilBERT applied it during general pre-training, with a combined "triple loss" of language modelling, distillation and cosine-distance terms [41]. It was trained on the same corpus as BERT (English Wikipedia and the Toronto Book Corpus) on eight 16-GB V100 GPUs for about 90 hours [41].

**Key numbers.** According to the authors, DistilBERT is 40 % smaller than BERT, keeps 97 % of its language-understanding capability and is 60 % faster [41]. Concretely, it has 66 million parameters versus 110 million for BERT-base; a full CPU inference pass over the STS-B task took 410 seconds versus 668; and on IMDb sentiment classification it reached 92.82 % accuracy versus 93.46 % for BERT-base [41]. For comparison, that is about one sixth of Laya's 421 million parameters [14], and a tiny fraction of any frontier LLM.

**Why it matters in the Jev debate.** A fine-tuned encoder like DistilBERT is, in effect, a typed decision model: for classification, a small output layer maps the representation to probabilities over a fixed label set. Its strengths are exactly Jev's selling points: speed, low cost and outputs that cannot fall outside the label set. The newsletter reports that a DistilBERT fine-tuned on up to 10,000 examples answers locally in under 10 ms instead of about 380 ms for Jev [1], and the pre-registered study found a comparable small encoder (bge-small plus logistic regression, 10,003 examples) at 9 ms with the best Banking77 accuracy of all tested systems [21]. The weakness is the flip side: a fine-tuned encoder needs labelled data for each task and must be retrained when the categories change, whereas Jev works zero-shot on any schema you define at call time; without labels, Jev was the stronger option in the same study [21].

**What this means for consultants.** The choice is less "Jev versus DistilBERT" than a question about the client's situation. With stable categories, thousands of labelled examples and strict data-residency needs, a small fine-tuned encoder that runs on the client's own infrastructure with no per-call fee is hard to beat [21]. With changing categories, no labels, or a need to start tomorrow, a zero-shot decision model is the pragmatic entry point. A combination is also plausible and is our own recommendation rather than an established result: start with a zero-shot model or an LLM, collect its decisions and human corrections as labels, and later replace it with a distilled small model for the high-volume cases. This corresponds to the "LLM approximation" strategy described in the FrugalGPT paper, i.e. building cheaper models that match an expensive model on a specific task [24].

## 11. Independent evaluations

**Phishing benchmark (single question vs. decomposition).** The reproducible `jev-phishing-bench` sends each of 2,000 e-mails from the PhishNChips benchmark to Jev with nine typed questions in one request and compares against Claude Haiku 4.5 without extended thinking [17]. On the single verdict question Jev reached 62.6 % accuracy versus 81.3 % for Haiku, AUROC 0.689 vs. 0.837 and ECE 0.154 vs. 0.097; median latency was 239 vs. 687 ms and cost USD 0.038 vs. 0.462 per 1,000 e-mails [18]. Jev caught only 43.2 % of phishing e-mails, Haiku 76.4 % [30]. Interestingly, the author notes that one of the narrow signal questions (free hosting) alone reaches AUROC 0.96, and a cross-validated logistic regression over five signals reaches 95.1 % [17]. With weights fitted on 1,000 labelled e-mails, the five-question setup reached 95.0 % on the other 1,000 [19]. A meta-review reports that a two-line regex scored 91.6 %, and that Haiku with the same five questions reached 93.2 %, a difference too small to be significant [20]. Network latency matters: 163 ms of Jev's 239 ms was network floor (measured from France) versus 18 ms for Haiku [18]; JevBench therefore warns that the 2.9× ratio does not transfer to other setups [23].

**Vendor claims vs. independent ratios.** TypeSafe advertises 193.6× faster and 444.6× cheaper [11]. An analysis points out that the 444.6× figure uses the average of GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1 as the reference answer, so it measures agreement with other models rather than accuracy [19]. Independent comparisons show 12 to 27 times lower cost than Haiku [19] and roughly five times lower latency than GPT-5.6 Terra [22].

**Intent classification and baselines.** A 17 September evaluation of Jev 1.13.0 vs. GPT-5.6 Terra on three tasks (300 samples each) found Banking77 intent accuracy of 0.78 vs. 0.85, SST-5 sentiment 0.57 vs. 0.59 and IMDB polarity 0.97 vs. 0.97; median latency 0.20 s vs. 1.04 s, and USD 0.04 vs. 2.02 per 1,000 intent calls [22]. The same report notes that Jev counts about twice as many input tokens for equivalent text, which reduces the price advantage [22]. A pre-registered evaluation on 18 September found a frozen `bge-small` encoder plus logistic regression, trained on 10,003 examples, scoring 0.933 on Banking77 at 9 ms and no per-call cost, ahead of both Jev (0.832) and a frontier model (0.875) [21][22]. The author's pragmatic conclusion: where labelled data exists, test this baseline before paying per call; without labelled data, Jev is a reasonable zero-shot router, 7.5 points better than the nano-class LLM tested [21]. Jev's confidence did not rank its own errors better than the LLM's verbalised confidence on CLINC150 (AUROC 0.734 vs. 0.816, not significant) [21].

**Cascades.** The pre-registered cascade experiment on Banking77/CLINC150 reported an escalation-rate effect whose 95 % confidence interval spanned zero [23], so the cascade benefit is plausible but not yet statistically established in independent work. A broader review of the first eight days summarises Jev as level with mid-priced LLMs and behind the frontier, with good rankings on familiar English tasks but large misses on unfamiliar ones; repeatability was imperfect, with 1.33 % and 2.2 % of answers changing between identical passes in two studies [20].

## 12. Calibration: theory and evidence

Calibration means that predicted probabilities match actual correctness rates. Guo et al. showed in 2017 that modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, and that a simple post-processing step, temperature scaling, is surprisingly effective [26]. This is exactly what Laya requires to reach its advertised calibration [14], and what the independent Jev studies recommend.

For Jev, the evidence so far is mixed. TypeSafe publishes no calibration error, reliability diagram, Brier score or log loss for any dataset; its only confidence-to-accuracy figure is a cookbook of 60 SEC filings (90 % correct at confidence ≥ 0.9, 40 % below) [20]. An independent study on synthetic support tickets measured an ECE of 0.107, about 4.4× the noise floor; Choice and Score answers were overconfident, yes/no answers underconfident, and on a question it could not know the answer to, Jev was right 44.7 % of the time at an average stated probability of 0.74 [19]. The recommended practice is to calibrate per question rather than per model and to run Jev in shadow mode on 1,000 to 2,000 decisions before trusting it [19]. On a small, hand-labelled tool-call risk set of 60 cases, ECE was 0.0712, but 50 of the 60 cases fell into the 0.9–1.0 bin, so the number is dominated by one bin [18]. Practitioners suggest that confidence thresholds should scale with risk: a read-only action may fire at a low threshold, a destructive one should wait for high confidence [11].

## 13. Counter-positions and critical perspectives

The main counter-arguments are: (a) accuracy has to be built through decomposition and labelled data, because single-question accuracy is often far below a cheap LLM and only decomposition plus labelled data closes the gap [19]; (b) where labelled data exists, a supervised small encoder or even a regex can match or beat Jev at zero marginal cost [21][20]; (c) headline speed and cost multipliers are vendor-run and depend on the baseline chosen [4][19]; (d) architecture, data and subsidies are opaque [33][4]; and (e) the idea is not new, as SalesRLAgent, FrugalGPT-style cascades and encoder classifiers show [12][24][34].

The supportive position is equally clear: Jev gives the decision steps in agent pipelines a tool that fits the job, rather than replacing generative models [32]; the recommended architecture is to let the generative model plan and write and the decision model score, verify and route [9]; and zero-shot, without any labelled data, it is a reasonable router [21]. TechCrunch quotes Armin Ronacher expecting competitors to appear now that the utility is visible [2], which the Laya release and the clones already confirm [14][34].

The newsletter author's concern about false security [1] is supported by the evidence that confidence can be high even when the model cannot know the answer [19] and that silent wrong answers are harder to detect than malformed ones [30].

## 14. Relevance for software consulting

**Architecture in client projects.** The most concrete takeaway is an architectural pattern rather than a product: separate "System 1" decisions (routing, classification, guardrails, relevance filtering, tool selection) from "System 2" generation and reasoning, and give each step the cheapest component that meets its quality bar [32][9]. This is the cascade idea formalised by FrugalGPT [24], now available as a hosted API. Typical candidates in our projects are ticket and e-mail triage, document classification in intake processes, pre-filtering for RAG pipelines, and safety checks before agent tool calls. TypeSafe's own guidance to keep arithmetic in code and to filter state before sending it [3] fits well with conventional software design: deterministic logic stays in code, only the judgement goes to the model.

**Delivery practice.** The independent evaluations suggest a clear engagement pattern: first establish baselines (regex, rules, a supervised encoder if labels exist) [20][21]; decompose vague questions into narrow, literal sub-questions [17][3]; calibrate thresholds per question on client data and run in shadow mode before going live [19]; and treat model outputs as testable artefacts in CI, as tools like `jevassert` already allow [31]. Because answers can change between identical passes [20], pinning a model version and building regression tests is important; TypeSafe's own tutorial recommends pinning e.g. `jev-1.13.0` for production [35].

**Advisory: build vs. buy, lock-in, data protection.** Jev is a closed, US-hosted API with undisclosed architecture and possibly subsidised pricing [33][4]; open alternatives like Laya can run locally but need fine-tuning and labelled data to be useful [14]. For clients with strict data-residency requirements, a local encoder or an open model may be preferable; routing through a gateway with zero-data-retention is another option [5]. Price stability should be treated as a risk in business cases.

**Advisory: regulation.** Fast, cheap, confident decisions invite automation of decisions that affect people. Under GDPR Article 22, decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects on individuals are restricted and require safeguards such as the right to human intervention [29]. Under the EU AI Act, the high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems were postponed by the Digital Omnibus to 2 December 2027, and for AI embedded in regulated products to 2 August 2028 [27]; the Omnibus was published as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and entered into force on 27 July 2026 [28]. The deadlines moved, but the obligations did not disappear, so use cases such as HR screening or credit decisions need a human-in-the-loop design from the start. This is the concrete, near-term version of the newsletter's worry about administration and military uses [1].

**Demo setup used in the talk.** The live demo runs Kev locally as a System-One-compatible server, so the same scripts can later target Laya or Jev by changing URL, model and key [58][50]. It has three parts: ticket triage with four typed questions in a single POST, the urgency decision inside SAP's CAP incident-management reference app, and a router that assigns coding tasks to Haiku, Sonnet or Opus [58]. In the reference app a handler sets urgency to high when the title matches the regex "urgent" [59]; the demo derives the allowed options from the CDS enum, acts only above a confidence threshold and keeps the regex as fallback, so the app's existing tests still pass [58]. Kev's confidence value is normalised as (p_max − 1/K) / (1 − 1/K), so with three options a threshold of 0.5 corresponds to a raw probability of about 0.67 [58]. The router escalates uncertain tasks one tier up and sends risky ones (authentication, personal data, deletion) to the largest model plus a human; its savings depend on the task mix and its quality impact still requires an eval [58], in line with the unproven cascade effect in independent work [23].

**Our own tooling.** Several community projects target developer workflows directly: coding-agent guardrails and an MCP server [5], command-safety review [4], and a Claude Code plugin for context compaction [1]. These are low-risk ways for us to gain hands-on experience internally before recommending the pattern to clients.

## 15. Contradictions and discrepancies between newsletter and research

- **Regex baseline:** The newsletter reports 91.8 % for the two-line regex [1]; the independent meta-review reports 91.6 % [20]. The difference is small and may stem from different runs or rounding, but the talk uses "≈ 92 %".
- **"Rund fünf Punkte hinter Terra" / 791 decisions:** I could not locate the specific 791-decision comparison. The independent evaluation I found (Banking77, 300 samples) shows a 7-point gap (0.78 vs. 0.85) and cost of USD 0.04 vs. 2.02 per 1,000 calls [22], which differs from the newsletter's 1.5 vs. 61 cents [1]. Both point in the same direction (slightly less accurate, much cheaper), but the exact figures depend on the evaluation.
- **DistilBERT vs. Jev in "JevBench":** The newsletter mentions a fine-tuned DistilBERT [1]; the pre-registered study I found used a frozen `bge-small` encoder with logistic regression [21]. The finding (supervised small encoder beats Jev when labels exist) is consistent; the specific DistilBERT result was not verified.
- **Pre-registered 0.99 threshold:** The newsletter's claim that Jev was always right at confidence 0.99 (60 % of requests) [1] could not be traced to a primary source in this research; it is presented in the talk as "reported by c't".
- **Speed:** The newsletter says "about three times as fast" [1], TypeSafe claims up to 193.6× [11]. Both are correct for their respective baselines; independent ratios range from about 3× to 5× end-to-end [18][22].
- **RLCD naming:** The newsletter and most sources say "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions" [1][8]; TechCrunch writes "from calibrated decisions" [2]. Cosmetic only.
- **Size of the ecosystem:** The newsletter counts around 25 open re-implementations on the awesome list [1]; a later catalogue counts about 35 open alternatives [52]. The difference reflects different dates and counting rules; the talk says "≈ 35".
- **"Architecture undisclosed":** Still true officially [33], but an outside black-box analysis now offers a plausible inferred architecture that clones such as Kev build on [49][50].
- **Kev model size in the demo (resolved 27 Sep):** The demo README uses a `kev-0.6b` checkpoint on a Qwen3 base [58], while the Kev repository README describes the Qwen3.5 models [50]. The checkpoint exists: it is the previous Qwen3 generation, kept as the fast option on Apple Silicon (0.12 s per five-question request vs. 0.33 s for Kev-0.8B), with slightly lower out-of-domain accuracy (0.642 vs. 0.668) [67].
- **Banking77 accuracy of Jev:** The pre-registered study used on the slides reports 0.832 zero-shot [21]; the jev-benchmarks pilot reports 0.870 on a 72-label BTZSC variant with 100 examples [62]. Different label sets, samples and prompts; the talk keeps 0.832 and mentions the spread.
- **Sign-up status:** Jev was opened without a waitlist on 21 September [64]; a pause of sign-ups after a demand surge is also reported [5][64]. On 27 September the TypeSafe homepage states that TypeSafe is invite-only again, with a waitlist for new users while existing users can still log in [69]. The talk uses the status of 27 September; check again shortly before 2 October.
- **Laya fine-tuning caveat** is confirmed [14]; **SalesRLAgent date (March 2025) and repository name** are confirmed [12][13].

Updates collected after the first version of this document. Each entry is dated; items are moved into the slides only after review.

### 27 Sep 2026, late

**Winnow-E4B.** EldanRing released Winnow-E4B, an Apache-2.0 LoRA fine-tune of Google's Gemma 4 E4B instruction model (about 8B parameters) that answers noul, choice and score questions through a `/v1/systemone` endpoint, like Jev [70]. The same merged weights also serve ordinary chat and image input, so one local model covers both the decision path and generation [70]. GGUF files run with llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio and vLLM; the recommended Q8 file is 8 GB and needs about 10 GB of GPU memory [70]. Reported results compare it only with the authors' own Winnow-12B, for example 80.5 % on a public JevBench subset and 72.7 % on the Kev v9 suite; there is no comparison with Jev [70]. The authors state that the public suites were known when the training data was refined, and training data and adapter are not released [70]. Assessment: a further example of the pattern being rebuilt on arbitrary base models, notable for combining System 1 and System 2 in one set of weights, but not important enough for the slides.

### 27 Sep 2026, evening

**Access closed again.** The TypeSafe homepage now states that TypeSafe is invite-only again: new users enter their e-mail address to join a waitlist, and existing users can still log in [69]. This reverses the open access from 21 September [64]. The user of this document received access on 27 September after waiting on the list; new users currently have to wait.

### 27 Sep 2026

**Availability and versions.** Since 21 September Jev is available to everyone without a waitlist, with 5 USD of starting credit equivalent to roughly 120 million tokens [64]. Jev is now versioned: OpenRouter lists Jev 1.13 and a "Jev Router" that picks a model and a reasoning effort per request and always points to the latest Jev model [61]. TypeSafe publishes a page of known weaknesses per model version, for example for jev-1.13 [65].

**Framework integrations.** Pydantic AI provides a dedicated model class for Jev and escalates to a language model where Jev cannot answer, for example for free-text fields [65]. LangChain offers a router middleware in which Jev picks between a fast and a capable model based on criteria set by the developer [66].

**Kev family.** Kev now spans 0.6B (previous Qwen3 generation, fast on Apple Silicon) to 27B, the latter as a LoRA adapter plus pointer head on a post-trained Qwen3.8-27B [67][68]. Kev's README compares selective automation with Jev: at a 5 % error budget Kev automates 0.45–0.57 of decisions, Jev 0.70; on knowledge questions the gap is larger (MMLU 0.74 for Kev-9B vs. 0.90 for Jev) [50].

**Ollaya.** An independent Apache-2.0 runtime that downloads and serves open decision models locally, on CPU or NVIDIA GPU, and implements TypeSafe's `/v1/systemone` API so the official SDK works unchanged [60]. It bundles Laya, Kev, decider, von, NLI and GLiClass models and Qwen3Guard [60]. Its own latency chart puts Laya at 8–10 ms on an RTX 4090 against 236–276 ms for the hosted Jev API; the project notes that setups differ and the comparison is an order of magnitude only [60].

**jev-benchmarks.** A pre-registered pilot with 300 examples compares Jev with GLiNER2.5 on zero-shot classification and reports, besides accuracy, calibration and "coverage at ≤ 5 % error" [62]. Jev is clearly ahead on AG News (0.910) and Banking77 (0.870) with coverage of 0.83 and 0.86; on DAIR Emotion accuracy is 0.48, coverage 0.00, and Jev assigned zero probability to the true label in 16 % of examples [62]. The authors call it a pilot, not a leaderboard, and note that thresholds were chosen on the same slice [62].

**Julia 1.** Supersonic Labs (Brazil) released a 144.3M-parameter decision model on mmBERT-small, Apache 2.0, running on CPU, trained for about 104 USD of GPU time [63]. On the jev-benchmarks protocol it reports 94 % on AG News and 86 % on DAIR Emotion against Jev reference values of 91 % and 48 %, but 64 % against 87 % on the 72-label Banking77 task; the Jev values are taken from the protocol, not re-measured [63]. A hosted API at 0.025 USD per million input tokens is announced [63].

**Noise.** One news aggregator mentions a TypeSafe model called "Zev" [64]; no other source supports this, and it is treated as a misspelling of Jev.

**Assessment.** The developments support the talk's thesis that the pattern matters more than the product: a local runtime, a 104-dollar model and native framework support appeared within two weeks. On selective automation, the measure closest to a business case, Jev still leads the open alternatives on the published numbers [50][62].
